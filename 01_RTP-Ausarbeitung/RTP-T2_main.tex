            \subsection{P-Glied}

                \subsubsection{Explanation}
                    Proportional-Glied, show a constant behavior in steady state for a constant input. The output is directly proportional to the input, with the proportionality constant being $K_P$.
                    The equation for a P-Glied is given by 
                    \begin{equation}
                        x_a(t) = K_P x_e(t)
                    \end{equation}
                    where $x_a(t)$ is the output and $x_e(t)$ is the input. The transfer function of a P-Glied is $G(s) = K_P$ \ref{eq:P}.

                \subsubsection{Parameters needed for characterization}
                    The only parameter needed to characterize a P-Glied is the proportional gain $K_P$. This parameter determines the steady-state gain of the system and how much the output will change in response to a change in the input.

                \subsubsection{Step and Impulse response}
                    The step response of a P-Glied is a step function that reaches a steady state value of $K_P$ times the input step magnitude. The impulse response is an impulse function scaled by $K_P$. Both responses are sketched in Figure~\ref{fig:P-Glied-responses}.

                    \begin{figure}[ht]
                        \centering
                        \input{figure/tikz/P-Glied-responses}
                        \caption{P-Glied: step response $h(t) = K_P \sigma(t)$ (left) and impulse response $g(t) = K_P\,\delta(t)$ (right).}
                        \label{fig:P-Glied-responses}
                    \end{figure}

            \subsection{PT$_1$-Glied}

                \subsubsection{Explanation}
                    The PT$_1$-Glied is a first-order delay system that consists of a proportional element and a first-order part. The transfer function of a PT$_1$-Glied is given by 
                    
                    \begin{equation}
                        T_1 \dot{x}_a + x_a(t) = K_P x_e(t)
                    \end{equation}

                    or equation \ref{eq:PT1}, where $K_P$ is the proportional gain and the final value of the function and $T_1$ is the time constant. 
                
                \subsubsection{Parameters needed for characterization}
                    For the characterization of a PT$_1$-Glied, the parameters needed are the proportional gain $K_P$ and the time constant $T_1$. The proportional gain $K_P$ determines the steady-state gain of the system, while the time constant $T_1$ determines how quickly the system responds to changes in the input.
                    The System is also characterized by its initial tangent, which is given by $y(t) = \frac{K_P}{T_1} t$. The point at which the initial tangent intersects the steady state value of the step response can be used to determine the time constant $T_1$. $T_1$ is the time it takes for the system to reach approximately $63\%$ of its final value in response to a step input. The threefold multiple of the time constant $T_1$ at which the system reaches $95\%$ of its final value can also be used to characterize the system's response speed. 

                \subsubsection{Step and Impulse response}
                    The step response of a PT$_1$-Glied is an exponential function that approaches a steady state value of $K_P$ times the input step magnitude, with a time constant of $T_1$. The impulse response is an exponential function scaled by $K_P/T_1$.

            \subsection{PT$_2$-Glied}

                \subsubsection{Explanation}
                %\todo[inline]{Check explanation of the PT2-Glied again, look into script}
                    
                    A PT$_2$-Glied is a second-order delay system that consists of a proportional element and a second-order part, which appears delayed and oscillatory behavior because of its second order differential nature. The transfer function of a PT$_2$-Glied is given by 
                    \begin{equation}
                        \frac{1}{\omega_0^2} \ddot{x}_a + \frac{2D}{\omega_0} \dot{x}_a + x_a(t) = K_P x_e(t)
                    \end{equation}
                    or equation \ref{eq:PT2_w0_D} , where $K_P$ is the proportional gain, $\omega_0$ is the natural frequency, and $D$ is the damping ratio.

                \subsubsection{Parameters needed for characterization}
                    For the characterization of a PT$_2$-Glied, the parameters needed are the proportional gain $K_P$, the natural frequency $\omega_0$, and the damping ratio $D$. The proportional gain $K_P$ determines the steady-state gain of the system, while the natural frequency $\omega_0$ and damping ratio $D$ determine the oscillatory behavior and response speed of the system. The damping ratio $D$ indicates whether the system is underdamped ($D < 1$), critically damped ($D = 1$), overdamped ($D > 1$) or undamped ($D = 0$).

                    For an underdamped system ($D < 1$), the step response will exhibit oscillations before settling to the steady state value. The natural frequency $\omega_0$ determines the frequency of these oscillations, while the damping ratio $D$ determines how quickly the oscillations decay. 
                    For a critically damped system ($D = 1$), the step response will reach the steady state value without oscillating, and it will do so as quickly as possible without overshooting. It is often reached by putting two PT$_1$-Glied in series, which results in the transfer function given by 
                    \begin{equation}
                        T_1T_2 \ddot{x}_a + (T_1 + T_2) \dot{x}_a + x_a(t) = K_P x_e(t)
                    \end{equation}
                    or 
                    \begin{equation}
                        h(t) = K_P \left(1 - (1 + \omega_0 t)e^{-\omega_0 t}\right)
                    \end{equation}
                    For an overdamped system ($D > 1$), the step response will reach the steady state value without oscillating, but it will take longer to do so than a critically damped system. The Transfer function can be rewritten as 
                    \begin{equation}
                        h(t) = K_P \left(1 + \frac{1}{\lambda_1 - \lambda_2}(\lambda_2e^{-\lambda_1t} - \lambda_1e^{-\lambda_2t})\right)
                    \end{equation} 
                    where $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ are the eigenvalues of the characteristic equation of the system, given by 
                    \begin{equation}
                        \lambda_{1,2} = -D\omega_0 \pm \omega_0\sqrt{D^2 - 1}
                    \end{equation}    
                    The time constants of the system can be determined from these roots, and they indicate how quickly the system responds to changes in the input.
                    For an undamped system ($D = 0$), the step response will exhibit sustained oscillations at the natural frequency $\omega_0$.

                    For the case $0 < D < 1$, the transfer function can be rewritten as 
                    \begin{equation}
                        h(t) = K_P \left(1 - \frac{e^{-\omega_0 D t}}{\sqrt{1 - D^2}} \sin(\omega_d t + \phi)\right)
                    \end{equation}

                    The Periodic time of the oscillations can be determined from the damped natural frequency 
                    \begin{equation}
                        T_d = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_d} = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_0 \sqrt{1 - D^2}}
                    \end{equation}
                    The transfer function can be characterized by the upper and lower envelope of the oscillations, which are given by 
                    \begin{equation}
                        h^\pm(t) = K_P \left(1 \pm \frac{e^{-D\omega_0 t}}{\sqrt{1-D^2}}\right)
                    \end{equation}
                    
                    The over swing time $t_{max}$, which is the time at which the first maximum of the step response occurs, can be determined from the equation 

                    \begin{equation}
                        t_{max} = \frac{\pi}{\omega_d} = \frac{\pi}{\omega_0 \sqrt{1 - D^2}}
                    \end{equation}

                    The overshoot $h_{max}$, can be determined from the equation 
                    \begin{equation}
                        h_{max} = h(t_{max}) = K_P \left(1 + e^{-\pi D/\sqrt{1-D^2}}\right)
                    \end{equation}
                    The normalized overshoot value $\ddot{u}$ can be determined from the equation
                    \begin{equation}
                        \ddot{u} = \frac{h_{max} - h(\infty)}{h(\infty)} = e^{\frac{-\pi D}{\sqrt{1-D^2}}}
                    \end{equation}
                    which can be used to determine the damping ratio $D$ from the overshoot of the step response. Given by the equation 
                    \begin{equation}
                        D = \frac{\left| \ln(\ddot{u}) \right|}{\sqrt{\pi^2 + \ln^2(\ddot{u})}}
                    \end{equation}

            \subsection{I-Glied}
                \subsubsection{Explanation}

                    Integral-Glied, are transfer functions that consist of an integrator of input signal, the output $x_a(t)$ is proportional to the integral of the input $x_e(t)$, with the proportionality constant being $K_I$. The transfer function of an I-Glied is given by $G(s) = \frac{K_I}{s}$ \ref{eq:I}. Can be rewritten as:
                    \begin{equation}
                        x_a(t) = K_I \int_0^t x_e(\tau) d\tau
                    \end{equation}
                    Equation \ref{eq:I} can also be rewritten as
                    \begin{equation}                    
                        h(t) = K_I \int_0^t \sigma(\tau) d\tau = K_I t
                    \end{equation}                 

                \subsubsection{Parameters needed for characterization}
                    The only parameter needed to characterize an I-Glied is the integral gain $K_I$. This parameter determines how quickly the output will change in response to a change in the input. The step response of an I-Glied is a ramp function that increases linearly with time, with a slope of $K_I$ times the input step magnitude. The impulse response is a step function scaled by $K_I$.

                \subsubsection{Step and Impulse response}
                    The step response of an I-Glied is a ramp function that increases linearly with time, with a slope of $K_I$ times the input step magnitude. The impulse response is a step function scaled by $K_I$. Both responses are sketched in Figure~\ref{fig:I-Glied-responses}.

                        \label{fig:I-Glied-responses}
                    \end{figure}

            \subsection{IT$_1$-Glied}
                \subsubsection{Explanation}
                    The IT$_1$-Glied is a system that consists of an integrator and a first-order part. The transfer function of an IT$_1$-Glied is given by $G(s) = \frac{K_I}{s (T_1 s + 1)}$ \ref{eq:IT1}, where $K_I$ is the integral gain and $T_1$ is the time constant. It could be seen as a combination of an I-Glied and a PT$_1$-Glied. The transfer function can be rewritten as

                    \begin{equation}
                        h(t) = K_I \left[t-T_1\left(1-e^{-t/T_1}\right)\right]
                    \end{equation} 

                \subsubsection{Parameters needed for characterization}
                    For the characterization of an IT$_1$-Glied, the parameters needed are the integral gain $K_I$ and the time constant $T_1$. The integral gain $K_I$ determines proportionality of the function to the integral of the input, while the time constant $T_1$ determines how delayed the response of the system is to changes in the input.

                \subsubsection{Step and Impulse response}
                    The step response of an IT$_1$-Glied is a function that increases with time, with a shape that is determined by the integral gain $K_I$ and the delay caused by the time constant $T_1$. The impulse response is a function that slowly (with a delay and a shape determined by $T_1$) increases from zero to the value of the integral of the input impulse scaled by $K_I$.

            \subsection{Realworld Example}

                A practical example of such a PT1 system with step excitation is a thermometer that is suddenly placed in a warm bath. The input signal $x_e(t)$ is the bath temperature, which jumps from the ambient value to the bath value as a step at the moment the sensor is immersed. The output signal $x_a(t)$ is the temperature read by the sensor, which does not follow the bath temperature instantaneously: heat must first flow through the sensor housing into the temperature-sensitive element, so the displayed temperature rises exponentially and asymptotically approaches the bath temperature.

                The proportional gain is $K_P = 1$ in this idealised case (in steady state the sensor reads exactly the bath temperature), and the time constant $T_1$ is set by the heat capacity of the sensor element divided by the thermal conductance between the bath and the element (housing material, wall thickness, contact area, convective heat transfer to the bath). After approximately $T_1$ the sensor has reached $63\,\%$ of the temperature jump, after $3 T_1$ approximately $95\,\%$. The same first-order behaviour appears in many other physical settings, for example RC low-pass filters under a step voltage or first-order mass-flow systems under a step inflow.

            \subsection{Documentation of the results}

                \subsubsection{Essential Commands}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Load measurement:}
    \begin{lstlisting}
data1 = np.loadtxt('data/sys1.csv', delimiter=',', skiprows=1)
    \end{lstlisting}

    \item \emph{Cumulative trapezoidal integration} (turns the impulse input into a step input and the impulse response into the corresponding step response):
    \begin{lstlisting}
cum = np.concatenate(([0.], np.cumsum(0.5 * (y[1:] + y[:-1]) * np.diff(t))))
    \end{lstlisting}

    \item \emph{Pulse area / step height:}
    \begin{lstlisting}
A_u = u_step[-1]
    \end{lstlisting}

    \item \emph{Static gain} from the integrated step response:
    \begin{lstlisting}
K_P = x_step_inf / A_u
    \end{lstlisting}

    \item \emph{First peak of the step response} ($\Rightarrow t_\mathrm{max}$, $x_\mathrm{max}$):
    \begin{lstlisting}
peak_idx = trigger_idx + np.argmax(x_step[trigger_idx:])
    \end{lstlisting}

    \item \emph{Overshoot:}
    \begin{lstlisting}
M_p = (x_peak - x_step_inf) / x_step_inf
    \end{lstlisting}

    \item \emph{Damping ratio} from the overshoot:
    \begin{lstlisting}
D = -np.log(M_p) / np.sqrt(np.pi**2 + np.log(M_p)**2)
    \end{lstlisting}

    \item \emph{Natural frequency} from $t_\mathrm{max}$, damped period $T_d$:
    \begin{lstlisting}
omega_0 = (np.pi / t_max) / np.sqrt(1 - D**2)
T_d = 2 * np.pi / (omega_0 * np.sqrt(1 - D**2))
    \end{lstlisting}

    \item \emph{PT2 step model} with phase $\varphi$:
    \begin{lstlisting}
phi = np.arctan2(np.sqrt(1 - D**2), D)
x_model = K_P * A_u * (1 - np.exp(-D * omega_0 * t) / np.sqrt(1 - D**2) * np.sin(omega_d * t + phi))
    \end{lstlisting}
\end{itemize}


                \subsubsection{Differential Equation}
                    The differential equation for a PT2 system can be derived from the transfer function and is given by
                    \begin{equation}
                        \frac{1}{\omega_0^2} \ddot{x}_a + \frac{2D}{\omega_0} \dot{x}_a + x_a(t) = K_P x_e(t)
                    \end{equation}

                \subsubsection{Transfer Function}
                    The Transfer function for the PT2 system can be derived from the differential equation and is given by
                    \begin{equation}
                        G(s) = \frac{K_P}{\frac{1}{\omega_0^2} s^2 + \frac{2D}{\omega_0} s + 1}
                    \end{equation}

            \subsection{Realworld Example}

                A classic example of a PT2 system with underdamped behaviour is a mass--spring--damper system excited by a step force. The input signal $x_e(t)$ is the applied force $F(t)$, which jumps from zero to a constant value at the moment of excitation. The output signal $x_a(t)$ is the displacement of the mass from its rest position, which does not jump immediately to the new equilibrium: the mass first overshoots, then performs damped oscillations around the steady-state value before settling.

                The equation of motion $m\,\ddot{x}_a + c\,\dot{x}_a + k\,x_a = F(t)$ matches the standard PT2 form with proportional gain $K_P = 1/k$ (the steady-state compliance, i.e.\ the inverse of the spring stiffness), natural frequency $\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}$ (set by mass $m$ and stiffness $k$), and damping ratio $D = c/(2\sqrt{mk})$ (set by the damping coefficient $c$ relative to the critical damping). For $0 < D < 1$ the response shows the characteristic overshoot and decaying oscillation, with a damped period $T_d = 2\pi/(\omega_0\sqrt{1 - D^2})$ and a first peak at $t_\mathrm{max} = \pi/(\omega_0\sqrt{1 - D^2})$. The same second-order behaviour appears in many other physical settings, for example a series RLC circuit driven by a step voltage (output: capacitor voltage, with $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$ and $D = (R/2)\sqrt{C/L}$), a moving-coil galvanometer responding to a step current, or a passive vehicle suspension reacting to a sudden load change.

Larger deviations are visible at the very beginning of the response: the excitation in the experiment is not an ideal Dirac impulse but a short triangular pulse of finite width, so the measured signal rises more gradually than the ideal impulse response and the first peak amplitude differs slightly from the model prediction. Once the impulse has passed, the damped oscillation matches the identified $D$, $\omega_0$ and $K_P$ within the noise level.                 \subsubsection{Differential Equation}
                    The differential equation for an IT1 system can be derived from the transfer function and is given by
                    \begin{equation}
                        T_1 \dot{x}_a + x_a(t) = K_I \int x_e(t) dt
                    \end{equation}

                \subsubsection{Transfer Function}
                    The transfer function for an IT1 system can be derived from the differential equation and is given by
                    \begin{equation}
                        G(s) = \frac{K_I}{s (T_1 s + 1)}
                    \end{equation}

            \subsection{Realworld Example}

                A clear example of an IT1 system is a water tank fed by an inflow $x_e(t)$ and drained through a linear resistance to a downstream sink. The inflow corresponds to the input signal, and the water level $x_a(t)$ to the output signal. The storage of the tank gives the system its integrative behaviour --- as long as the inflow exceeds the outflow, the level keeps rising --- while the proportional outflow causes a first-order lag with time constant $T_1 = A/k$, where $A$ is the cross-sectional area of the tank and $k$ is the linear hydraulic conductance of the outflow.

                The integral gain is $K_I = 1/A$ (a unit inflow integrates into a unit volume change, divided by the cross-section to obtain a level change). After a step change in inflow the level first lags behind for a duration of order $T_1$ and then approaches a straight ramp of slope $K_I\,\Delta x_e$. The same IT1 behaviour appears in other accumulating systems with first-order losses, for example a heated body with a fixed heat input and a proportional convective loss to the ambient, or a capacitor charged through a current source while leaking through a parallel resistor.
